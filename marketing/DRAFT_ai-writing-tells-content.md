## 1. Long-form piece (for a Journal/Our Voice page, or a reworked chapter)

**Why We Don't Let a Machine Write These Chapters**

I read a chapter draft last month that felt off before I could say why. Every sentence moved the same way. Somewhere around the fourth paragraph there was a "quiet resilience" and a stat about footfall with no source behind it. I checked — the number wasn't wrong, exactly. It just wasn't ours. Nobody at Pahari Yatri had counted anything.

That's when I started paying closer attention to how writing gives itself away, not just here, but everywhere I read now.

**What we started noticing**

The tells are small on their own. "It's not X, it's Y" shows up in a paragraph and you don't think much of it. Then it shows up again two paragraphs later, and a third time before the piece ends, and you realize you weren't reading — you were being handed the same trick three times and asked to be surprised each time.

Same with the rule of three. Three examples, three reasons, three takeaways, every single section, until the piece stops sounding like thinking and starts sounding like a template someone forgot to vary. Same with the sentences that all open the same way — "It's worth noting," "It's important to remember" — you don't consciously count them, but something in you starts to disengage anyway.

The one that bothers me most is the invented case study. A "local guide named Sohan Lal" who says something a little too tidy, a little too on-message, and doesn't exist anywhere outside the paragraph he was written into. We work with real guides. Some of them can't read the chapters we write about their own villages, because they never learned English past what tourism required of them. Inventing a quote in their voice isn't a shortcut — it's a small theft.

**Why this matters more for us than for most sites**

A travel brand that gets caught writing generic scenic copy loses some credibility. A brand that writes about devta rituals, about which trail is safe in September and which one floods, about who actually still lives in a village versus who's shown in the photos — that brand is making claims people will act on. Someone reads what we write about Kamrunag or a route past Kheerganga and then walks there. If the "local truth" in that chapter came from a language model's best guess instead of someone who's actually stood at that lake, the gap between what we said and what's true becomes someone else's problem, on a trail, possibly in weather we didn't warn them about.

So the standard isn't "does this read well." It's "who told us this, and would they stand behind it with their name on it."

**How the check actually works**

Every chapter that touches a local belief, a ritual, a devta story, or a claim about a place goes through a separate pass before it's allowed to publish. Each claim gets sorted into one of three buckets: verified, meaning we have a named source who confirmed it; needs-local-source, meaning it's plausible but nobody's confirmed it yet, so it doesn't run; or unsafe, meaning it gets rewritten or cut, full stop. That's not a suggestion pass. It blocks publishing.

Practically, that means a chapter with an unverified claim sits unfinished longer than a competitor's chapter would. We've shipped slower because of this more than once. I don't think that's a cost. I think it's the actual product.

It also means our writing sounds less smooth in places, and I've made peace with that. A real quote from a homestay owner doesn't land as cleanly as an invented one would. A route description hedged with "as of last season, this changed" reads less confident than a flat, tidy sentence claiming certainty nobody actually has. Good. Certainty we don't have shouldn't sound confident.

**What we watch for, specifically**

A few patterns that get flagged the moment they show up in a draft, ours or anyone else's:

Stats with no source or no date attached — a number sitting on the page asking to be believed on faith. Quotes attributed to someone we can't name and haven't actually spoken to. A cadence so even it stops sounding like a person — no rough edges, no place where the writer clearly changed their mind mid-paragraph, no digression. Real writing has friction in it. A route description that reads like every other route description, right down to the sentence structure, because it was generated from the same pattern as everything around it instead of walked.

None of this means a tool can't help draft something. It means the draft doesn't get to stand on its own. A person who's either been to the place or spoken directly to someone who has needs to put their name behind every factual and cultural claim before it goes live. That's the actual bar, and it isn't a metaphor — there's a literal named-source requirement in our editorial process, and it's checked every time.

**What this costs us, honestly**

Slower publishing. Fewer chapters per month than we could technically produce. Some sections that read a little rougher than a fully generated piece would. A few claims we've wanted to make about a place that we've had to leave as "needs-local-source" and hold back, because nobody had actually confirmed them yet, even though they sounded true.

I'd rather have a shorter library that's actually ours than a long one I can't stand behind sentence by sentence.

**FAQ**

**Does Pahari Yatri use AI at all in its writing process?**
Tools can help with drafting and structure. Nothing about a place, a ritual, or a local claim goes live without a named human source behind it, checked through a separate verification pass before publishing.

**How do you verify a claim about a devta, temple, or local custom?**
Every such claim is checked against a real, named local source — someone who lives there, has worked there, or has direct knowledge — before it's marked verified. If no source can be found, the claim doesn't run as fact; it's either hedged or cut.

**What happens if a claim turns out to be wrong after it's published?**
It gets corrected and re-sourced, the same as any factual error would. We'd rather run a hedge than a confident wrong answer, but mistakes still happen, and the fix is the same either way — find the real source, fix the text.

## 2. LinkedIn post (founder-led draft — not posted, counts toward the 1/week cap if used)

I've started reading everything twice now. Once for what it says, once for whether a person actually wrote it.

Some tells I keep running into: "it's not X, it's Y" showing up in every third paragraph. Case studies about someone named Sarah Chen who, as far as I can tell, does not exist. Stats with no source and no date, just sitting there asking to be believed. Sentences that all move the same way, like they came off the same machine — because they did.

None of this makes AI writing bad. It makes a lot of it forgettable, which might be worse.

It's part of why every chapter on Pahari Yatri gets written and checked by a person who's actually been to the place, or has talked to someone who has. Not because a tool couldn't produce something readable — it could, easily — but because a devta ritual or a village route deserves a named source, not a confident guess dressed up as fact.
