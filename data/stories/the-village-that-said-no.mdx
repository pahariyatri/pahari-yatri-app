I had packed a small bottle of rum for the climb to Grahan the way I pack one for most treks, a habit from years of cold camps and tired evenings. Somewhere around the second hour of the walk, a signboard nailed to a tree made the village's one rule plain: no alcohol, enforced by fine, no exceptions made for visitors.

I kept walking, mildly annoyed in the way you get annoyed at a rule that inconveniences a habit rather than a value. By the time I reached the village, the annoyance had mostly worn off into curiosity.

My homestay host, an older man with the unhurried manner of someone who has explained this exact thing many times, told the story the way it is apparently always told here: a vision, shared by the whole village at once, a decision made and kept without needing to be revisited since. I do not know how literally to take the vision part. I know the rule has clearly outlived whoever first proposed it, which is its own kind of proof that a community actually believes in something.

I did not open the bottle. Nobody was watching to check. That, more than the signboard, was what stayed with me, a rule that holds not because of enforcement but because the whole village has simply decided together that it will.

I carried the rum back down two days later, still sealed, and gave it to a friend in Kasol who did not ask why.
